Abstract—This paper presents a novel framework for develop-
ing autonomous cyber defense agents that incorporate defender
goals into their decision-making processes. Evaluated within
the CAGE Challenge 2 simulation environment, the framework
enables agents to adapt their strategies to align with specific
objectives, such as maximizing rewards or deploying decep-
tion. Leveraging previously validated strategies, the proposed
approach enhances the adaptability and effectiveness of cyber
defense systems, providing a foundation for addressing evolving
threats in dynamic cyber environments.
I. I NTRODUCTION
The rise in the frequency and sophistication of cyberattacks
has underscored the critical need for advanced defensive strate-
gies capable of protecting enterprise networks. Autonomous
Cyber Defense (ACD) systems have emerged as a promising
solution, which uses artificial intelligence to counter adver-
sarial threats in dynamic and complex environments. Within
this domain, the Cyber Autonomy Gym for Experimentation
(CAGE) Challenge 2 has become a benchmark environment
to evaluate the performance of autonomous agents to defend
enterprise networks against attackers [1].
The CAGE Challenge 2 environment, developed as part
of the Cyber Operations Research Gym (CybORG) initiative,
provides a simulation framework for post-exploitation scenar-
ios. This environment includes a network comprising user,
enterprise, and operational subnets, each of varying strategic
importance. The challenge tasks Blue agents with defending
these subnets from Red agents, which employ strategies such
as reconnaissance, exploitation, and escalation. Key features
of CAGE Challenge 2 include a diverse state-action space,
configurable decoy mechanisms, and comprehensive scoring
metrics to evaluate the effectiveness of defensive strategies.
Enhancements to the original CybORG framework, as exem-
plified in CybORG++, have improved the scalability, trans-
parency, and reliability of the simulation, enabling more robust
experimentation with reinforcement learning-based defensive
agents [2].
Despite the advancements made in the development of
Blue agents for the CAGE Challenge 2 environment, existing
methodologies often lack a systematic framework to guide
their design and alignment with specific defensive goals. While
efforts such as hierarchical reinforcement learning [4], ensem-
ble methods, and heuristic strategies have shown promise, the
lack of a unified approach has limited the reproducibility and
extensibility of these agents.
In this paper, we propose a novel framework for the sys-
tematic generation of Blue agents in the CAGE Challenge 2
environment. Our framework is designed with two primary
principles in mind: (i) incorporating the goals of the defender
into the decision-making process and (ii) grounding defensive
strategies in previously vetted approaches that have shown
effectiveness in past scenarios. This alignment ensures that
the agents produced by our framework are both purposeful and
adaptable, capable of addressing evolving threats and dynamic
environments. Moreover, our framework can accommodate the
evolution of defender goals over time, allowing seamless mod-
ification of strategies to meet shifting priorities or adversarial
tactics.
Although our framework is validated within the context of
the CAGE Challenge 2 environment, its design and principles
may have broader applicability to real-world autonomous
cyber defense. The ability to encode defender objectives and
adapt to changing dynamics makes this approach particularly
well suited for a wide range of cybersecurity challenges,
including those beyond the scope of the CAGE Challenge
2 simulation. This work advances the state-of-the-art in au-
tonomous cyber defense and establishes a replicable method-
ology for developing agents that align defensive strategies
with organizational goals and evolving operational needs.
Our contributions aim to bridge the gap between theoretical
frameworks and practical implementation, fostering innovation
in goal-driven, adaptive defense mechanisms.
II. R ELATED WORKS AND CONTRIBUTIONS

driven the development of advanced AI-driven frameworks
to address evolving challenges. Among these, the concept
of Large Language Model (LLM)-based agents has gained
significant traction due to their ability to reason, plan, and
interact with dynamic environments. Central to our work
is the foundation laid by Moskal et al. [3], which demon-
strates the transformative potential of LLMs in automating
cyber threat testing and simulating adversarial activities. Their
framework highlights the ability of LLM-supported agents
to autonomously execute complex cyber campaigns, leverag-
ing prompt engineering to navigate intricate decision-making

The role of LLM agents in enhancing both offense and
defense within cybersecurity environments is further explored
in several studies. For instance, Rigaki et al. [9] introduced

system highlights the practical utility of LLMs in formulating

Our work also draws inspiration from the orchestrated use
of LLMs with external tools and specialized modules. Cui
et al. [11] introduced LLMind, a framework that employs
LLMs to coordinate complex tasks in IoT environments.
By integrating domain-specific AI modules and employing
finite state machines for task execution, LLMind demonstrated
how modularity and adaptability enhance the effectiveness of
autonomous agents. Similarly, Kaheh et al. [12] showcased
Cyber Sentinel, a dialogue-based agent that interprets potential
cyber threats and executes security actions in real time, im-
proving operational efficiency and threat response capabilities.
In contrast to the offensive potential of LLMs, as demon-
strated by Fang et al. [13] in their exploration of autonomous
website hacking, our research focuses on leveraging LLMs to
bolster defense. While these offensive capabilities illustrate the
dual-edged nature of LLM technology, they also underscore
the urgent need for robust, adaptive defensive frameworks
like the one we propose. The contributions of this work are
as follows: (i) We introduce a flexible framework, grounded
in previously validated strategies, that integrates defender
goals directly into the decision-making process, and (ii) We
demonstrate, within the CAGE simulation environment, that
the resulting agents exhibit behaviors consistent with the
defender’s specified goals.
III. CAGE C HALLENGE 2
CAGE Challenge 2 is a cybersecurity simulation frame-
work designed to evaluate and compare the performance of
defensive (blue) and offensive (red) agents within a networked
environment. This challenge utilizes the CybORG platform,
which provides a controlled environment to test autonomous
cyber defense strategies.
In the CAGE Challenge 2 scenario, the blue agent is tasked
with defending a manufacturing plant’s network belonging to
the nation of Florin, which is under cyber-attack from the
rival nation Guilder. The network comprises three subnets:
(i) Subnet 1: Contains non-critical user hosts, (ii) Subnet
2: Houses enterprise servers supporting user activities, (iii)
Subnet 3: Includes critical operational servers essential for
manufacturing processes.
The red agent begins with an initial foothold in subnet 1 and
attempts to move laterally through the network to compromise
the critical Operational Server in subnet 3. The blue agent’s
objective is to detect and mitigate these intrusions to maintain
the integrity and availability of the operational systems.
The performance of the blue agent is evaluated based on a
reward system that reflects the effectiveness of its defensive
actions. More specifically, the blue reward when the red
agent gains administrative access is −0.1 for low-importance
systems and −1 for high importance systems. If red executes
the impact action on the Operational Server then blue receives
a reward of −10. If blue performs a restore action, which
requires that a system be brought offline for some duration
of time, they are penalized −1. The cumulative reward over
the course of the scenario run determines the blue agent’s
final score. We refer to each scenario run as an episode and
each episode consisted of 30 rounds where both blue and red
execute one of their actions at every round.
The blue agent has a set of high-level actions to defend the
network that include:
• Analyze: Conduct detailed analysis on hosts to detect
malicious activities.
• Remove: Eliminate malicious software from hosts.
• Restore: Revert a host to a clean state from backup,
ensuring removal of entrenched threats.
• Misinform: Deploy decoy services to deceive attackers
and gather intelligence.
Each action has associated costs in terms of effort and potential
system downtime, requiring the blue agent to balance these
factors against the necessity of protecting various systems.
For additional information into CAGE and the CybORG
simulation environment, we refer the interested reader to [1].
In order to evaluate the effectiveness of different types of
blue agents, two red agents were included as part of the
challenge: (i) Meander Agent: Explores the network method-
ically, gaining privileged access on all hosts within a subnet
before proceeding to the next, and (ii) B-Line Agent: Utilizes
prior knowledge of the network layout to directly target the
Operational Server.
IV. A RCHITECTURE AND PROMPT ENGINEERING
As discussed in the previous section, the blue agent in
the CAGE simulation environment must select an action to
execute during each round of the simulation. Our approach
to enabling this functionality can be viewed as a variation of
the LLM-centric Planner-Actor-Reporter framework originally
introduced in [14]. This framework aligns with the OODA
(Observe, Orient, Decide, Act) loop, guiding the agent’s
observation and action processes. Our instantiation of this
framework cycles through the following two stages:
1) Action Retrieval: During this stage, the LLM accesses
a set of datastores containing potential actions.
2) Action Selection: Given a set of potential actions along
with some other additional information, the LLM is
asked to select an action that can be executed.

This process is illustrated in more detail in Figure 1.
The agent executes in rounds where each round of the
agent execution corresponds to a round of the CAGE scenario.
The round begins with the action retrieval stage, which takes
as input state information that is extracted from the CAGE
simulation environment. The state information is then used
to retrieve from a collection of cyber playbooks, which were
developed from the existing submissions to the challenge, a
set of actions that have been taken in previous games given
a similar state. The output of this stage is a set of potential
actions that will be combined into a prompt. In the next stage,
the LLM selects from the collection of potential actions the
action that best aligns with the defender’s stated goals and
information about the previous observation from the game
itself. Each round terminates with an action being selected
and then executed in the CAGE simulation environment.
One of the challenges in designing our agent architecture
stemmed from the stateless nature of the LLM itself. In
particular, the LLM has no memory between the prompts
provided as input, and consequently contextual information
about prior interactions must be provided to the LLM at
every round. Compounding this challenge is the fact that
existing LLMs typically limit the sizes of the prompts to be
around 4096 tokens (∼3K words). In order to overcome these
challenges, we made the following design decisions:
1) We initially developed a set of cyber playbooks that
are based upon previous submissions to the CAGE
Challenge 2 scenario. These playbooks allow us to focus
the attention mechanisms of the LLM in the second stage
on existing policies. By recommending potential actions,
we are able to reduce some of contextual information
required in the second stage since this information is
already previously used to retrieve information in the
first stage. Under our setup, in the second stage the
agent simply needs to decide between previously vetted
actions/policies.
2) We carefully construct our prompts following the gen-
eral template in [3]. This format is similar in spirit to
a single round of the well-known Reasoning and Ac-
tion (ReAct) framework, which has received significant
attention in the past [15].
Next, we turn our attention to discussing in more detail the
design of the two stages of our agent architecture.
A. Action Retrieval Stage
The key architectural component of the action retrieval
stage is the agent datastore. Our aim was to first construct
a capability that mimics the behavior of an individual agent
submitted to the CAGE Challenge. For shorthand let X denote
an agent submitted to CAGE. Our approach was to first
observe for a certain interval of time (in this case 30 rounds)
the behavior of each agent across a series of simulations. For
each round r ∈ [30] = {1, ..., 30}, we recorded an individual
memory where each memory consisted of the following two
pieces of information: (i) The action taken at round r, and (ii)
The previous m observations where m ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}.

One of the surprising findings we confirmed during our
process was that the datastores appeared to encapsulate all
the essential information needed to replicate the behavior
of each agent against both B-line and Meander opponents.
To demonstrate this, we created a set of four “mimicry”
agents—one for each of Cardiffuni, Mindrake, Keepin’ it RL -
Ensemble, and Keepin’ it RL - Transfer. For each agent X and
a given state, we utilized LlamaIndex [5] to store and retrieve
the most similar states encountered previously. The retrieved
information was used to determine the actions previously taken
in similar states, enabling the mimicry agents to replicate
the behavior of the original agents. The embeddings for state
representation were generated using SecBERT, a BERT-based
model pre-trained on cybersecurity-specific text, integrated
with the HuggingFace Transformers library [6]. The similarity
between states was calculated using the cosine distance metric.
When a datastore agent record is retrieved, the system
accesses the actions stored in its metadata. The next action
is then determined by sampling from the categorical proba-
bility distribution associated with the corresponding datastore
record. For example, if the record shown in Figure 2 is
retrieved for a given state, there is a 63% probability that
after sampling the resulting action “Remove User3” will be
selected.

A key observation is that while our mimicry agents demon-
strated behavior closely aligned with their original counterparts

differences in behavior across the various mimicry agents.
For example, Cardiffuni executed the “Remove” command an
average of about two times every 30 rounds against Meander,
whereas Mindrake almost never used the “Remove” command.
This diversity in behavior among the agents is crucial and
will be leveraged to generate a range of behavioral responses
tailored to the defender’s objectives.

B. Action Selection Stage
For the action selection stage, we utilized a standardized
template for crafting prompts to elicit responses from the
LLM. Following the approach outlined in [3], each prompt
can be broken down into three key components:
1) Setup: Provides a high-level overview of the environ-
ment and the objective the LLM is tasked with achiev-
ing.
2) Instruction: Clearly specifies the desired output, includ-
ing the format in which the LLM should deliver its
response.
3) Context: Supplies relevant background information that
the LLM can use to accurately generate responses based
on the instructions.
This structured approach ensures consistency and clarity in the
LLM’s interaction and decision-making process.

prompts is shown in Figure 4. Note that, for illustration

V. R ESULTS
In this section, we evaluate the performance of the LLM
agent architecture described in the previous section within the
CAGE simulation environment. Specifically, we examine the
behavior of five LLM agents, each distinguished by a variation
in the final sentence of the setup sub-prompt from Section IV-
B (see Figure 4), which begins with the phrase, “The primary
goal for the defender is to . . . ”. The variations are as follows:
1) No Goal : In this configuration, no specific goal is
provided to the model in the setup sub-prompt. The
sentence beginning with “The primary goal. . . ” (see
Figure 4) is omitted entirely.
2) Minimize Restore: “The primary goal for the defender
is to minimize the use of Restore.”
3) Minimize Infiltration Penalty: “The primary goal for
the defender is to minimize the Infiltration Penalty.”
4) Maximize Reward : “The primary goal for the defender
is to maximize the Reward Mean.”
5) Maximize Deception: “The primary goal for the de-
fender is to maximize the use of deception.”
Notice that the Minimize Restore and Maximize Deception
goals are directly the result of actions that are taken by
blue whereas the Maximize Reward and Minimize Infiltration
Penalty depend on the actions of both the red and blue agent
in the CAGE simulation environment.
To better understand the behaviors exhibited by our agents,
we collected and analyzed statistics from evaluations of all five
LLM agents against both Meander and B-line. Specifically, we
considered: (i) Reward Mean: The average reward per game,
presented as the negative of blue’s reward for consistency with
the other metrics, as in Figure 3. (ii) Num Decoys: The average
number of decoys deployed per episode throughout the sim-
ulation. (iii) Num Compromised Hosts: The average number
of actively compromised hosts per episode. (iv) Restore: The
average number of restore calls made per episode.
The results, based on running each of the five LLM agents
for 500 episodes of 30 rounds each in the CAGE simulation
environment, are displayed in Figure 5. Notice that there is
considerable variation between the different types of agents
and how they perform against Meander and B-line. For in-
stance, Maximize Deception deployed nearly 26 decoys per
round against Meander and almost 23 per round against B-
line, whereas Maximize Reward deployed less than 13 against
B-line and around 7 against Meander.
In general, the behavior of the agents was consistent with the
stated goal. For instance, against both B-Line and Meander,
the Maximize Deception agent deployed significantly more
decoys on average than the other agents under consideration.
In addition, the Maximize Reward agent consistently achieved
the best possible reward, although note that the reward in this
case was still less than the Cardiffuni mimicry agent discussed
in Figure 3. One of the possible reasons for this disparity is
likely due to the probabilistic nature of the model itself and
in particular due to the fact that a small fraction of the time
the model chose an action that did not necessarily align with
the stated goal in the setup sub-prompt, and in this case led
to the occasional selection of actions with lower rewards.
One of the surprising outcomes that arose from our simula-
tions is that not only did our LLM agents behave in accordance
with the provided goals for the goals that depended on blue
actions only, but the LLM agents also achieved goals that
depend upon both blue and red actions (i.e., the maximize
reward and minimize infiltration penalty goals).
As for performance against Meander and B-line specifically,
many of the agents were comparable but had a few notable
differences. The starkest difference was in minimize infiltra-
tion penalty agent’s reward mean being near best against B-

scenarios where the defender’s objective may shift during an
episode. Figure 6 presents statistics for two LLM agents, each
simulated for 500 episodes of 30 rounds:
1) Maximize Reward/Maximize Deception (MR/MD): At

objective is to maximize reward or maximize deception.

more decoys compared to the MR/MR agents, while the
MR/MR agents did not make fewer restore calls. Interestingly,
prioritizing reward maximization and the use of deception
resulted in a better overall mean reward. This suggests that,
in this game, employing deception may be a more effective
strategy than minimizing restore actions.
VI. C ONCLUSION
In this work, we presented a flexible framework for develop-
ing autonomous cyber defense agents that align their behavior
with specific defender goals. Through evaluation in the CAGE
Challenge 2 environment, we demonstrated that the agents
effectively adapt their strategies, such as maximizing rewards
or deploying deception, to meet these goals. This provides a
foundation for future research into adaptive, goal-driven cyber
defense systems capable of addressing evolving threats.
